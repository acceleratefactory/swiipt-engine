ON-SCREEN TEXT (deterministic; short lines only)

[The plan was always going to break some nights]
  If you are watching this at 3 AM because the plan collapsed, the first thing to know is that this is not evidence the plan was wrong.
  A plan that survives every night is not a robust plan; it is a plan that has not met a sick baby yet.
[Check the baby first]
  Before anything else, check the baby.
  If the baby has a fever, is vomiting, is showing signs of unusual pain, is having difficulty breathing, or you have any concern about their physical wellbeing, suspend the roster and follow your paediatric guidance or contact emergency services.
[The bad-night fallback]
  When a night collapses, the protocol is three lines long.
  Default to whoever's turn it technically is - do not re-litigate the fairness of the whole system at 3 AM.
[The four ways it usually breaks]
  There are four common failures, and each has a specific response.
  A missed shift - your partner slept through it - gets a no-blame reset: it is one data point, not a violation, so you adjust the alarm or the handoff method rather than the relationship.
[The no-blame reset]
  The most important word in the whole protocol is 'data'.
  If a missed shift becomes a character judgement, the system stops being usable, because now every failure costs a fight.
[How to review a bad night without a fight]
  The day after a bad night, do not review it in the middle of the day when you are both tired and busy.
  Put it at the scheduled weekly review, or at the five minutes before bed.
[When a helper arrives with old advice]
  One failure point deserves its own line, because it catches households out after the hardest part is over: a new helper - a relative, a visiting mother, a paid night nurse - arrives with their own idea of whose job the night is.
  The rule is simple and not personal: they read the shift chart before their first night shift.
[When exhaustion needs more than a night plan]
  One more thing, and it is the most important thing in this episode.
  Severe, sustained exhaustion can mask or worsen postpartum mental-health conditions, and a better roster will not fix that.
