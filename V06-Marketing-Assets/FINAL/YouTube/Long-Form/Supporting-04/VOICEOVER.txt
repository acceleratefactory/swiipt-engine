NARRATION (verbatim - no AI voice assumed, no invented claims)
Deliver calmly, at a normal pace. No hype, no urgency, no advertisement tone.

The 3 AM argument almost never happens because of the 3 AM situation. It happens because a decision that should have been made at 8 PM was never made at all. By 3 AM you are not deciding a roster; you are deciding who is more tired. That is not a decision anyone can make well.

The fridge chart is deliberately unglamorous. It is two clock times and one name per block, written by hand, in the same place every day. If tonight is 10 PM to 2 AM for one adult and 2 AM to 6 AM for the other, that is the whole chart. Its power is not detail. Its power is that it is already decided.

An app can hold more information than a sheet of paper. But information you have to open is information you will renegotiate. The chart goes on the fridge because it is in the path of both adults and neither of you has to be motivated to look at it. Visibility beats completeness.

The way it holds a real household: the working parent names the block they can actually keep, usually one before their own sleep or one after it. The other parent names the block around the feed hand-offs. The weekend gets the larger block, and the sleep banker records any night where one adult carried more than the chart. That is it - three lines and a credit.

Before the night starts, you spend five minutes on it. Who is on tonight, do the times still make sense, and is there anything unusual tomorrow - an early start, a trip, a hard day? Adjust tonight only. Do not redesign the system at night; adjust the chart before it.

Most charts break in one of three places. They become too detailed, so nobody fills them in. They live only in one adult's head, which puts the planning labour back on that person. Or they are treated as permanent, so the first week the situation changes, the chart becomes wrong and gets abandoned instead of redrawn. Keep it to two times and one name, put it where both of you will see it, and expect to redraw it weekly.

Do not judge the chart on night one. The first nights feel strange precisely because the decision is no longer yours to make in the moment - and if you are the parent who has been carrying every night, handing over can feel harder than staying up. Give it a full week and review it on day seven with three questions: how many nights had a protected block, what did exhaustion look like, and what is the one thing to change. Then redraw it.

The chart is not a contract carved in stone; it is a decision that gets re-decided weekly, because the baby's sleep pattern changes and so does your work. The rule is that renegotiating is expected and scheduled - never improvised at 3 AM.
