Deliver calmly, at a normal pace. No hype, no urgency, no advertisement tone.

If you are watching this at 3 AM because the plan collapsed, the first thing to know is that this is not evidence the plan was wrong. A plan that survives every night is not a robust plan; it is a plan that has not met a sick baby yet. The system expects bad nights. It has a protocol for them.

Before anything else, check the baby. If the baby has a fever, is vomiting, is showing signs of unusual pain, is having difficulty breathing, or you have any concern about their physical wellbeing, suspend the roster and follow your paediatric guidance or contact emergency services. The roster never takes priority over the baby's health. It resumes when the baby is stable and you have support in place.

When a night collapses, the protocol is three lines long. Default to whoever's turn it technically is - do not re-litigate the fairness of the whole system at 3 AM. The override triggers automatic next-night compensation, which you log. And you do not redesign anything tonight; you just run the fallback. Tomorrow, in daylight, you look at what broke.

There are four common failures, and each has a specific response. A missed shift - your partner slept through it - gets a no-blame reset: it is one data point, not a violation, so you adjust the alarm or the handoff method rather than the relationship. Outright refusal gets the 'trial it for one week' framing. Overwhelm - you cannot even follow your own half - gets the simplified version: one non-negotiable block, everything else dropped temporarily. A relapse, where you quietly slide back, restarts at the negotiate step with no guilt.

The most important word in the whole protocol is 'data'. If a missed shift becomes a character judgement, the system stops being usable, because now every failure costs a fight. If it stays a data point, you can fix the alarm and try again tomorrow. That is why the tracker records facts only - who had which block, whether the block was three hours, whether the partner initiated without a reminder - and not who was in the wrong.

The day after a bad night, do not review it in the middle of the day when you are both tired and busy. Put it at the scheduled weekly review, or at the five minutes before bed. Then ask three questions in this order: what happened, what made it worse, and what is the one mechanical thing we change. Mechanical - not character. 'The alarm was too quiet' is fixable today. 'You don't care' is not a plan, and it costs you the system.

One failure point deserves its own line, because it catches households out after the hardest part is over: a new helper - a relative, a visiting mother, a paid night nurse - arrives with their own idea of whose job the night is. The rule is simple and not personal: they read the shift chart before their first night shift. If they are covering a block, they follow the same guardrails as everyone else in the house.

One more thing, and it is the most important thing in this episode. Severe, sustained exhaustion can mask or worsen postpartum mental-health conditions, and a better roster will not fix that. This system does not diagnose anything. But if you feel hopeless, empty or sad in a way that does not lift even after a good sleep block, or you get panic or rapid heartbeats thinking about the night, or you cannot sleep even when someone else is on duty, or you have frightening intrusive thoughts, or you feel disconnected from your baby - that is not a scheduling problem. If two or more of those show up consistently for more than two weeks, seek professional assessment. If anything there is acute, act today.
