Deliver calmly, at a normal pace. No hype, no urgency, no advertisement tone.

The first thing to accept is that there is no single correct way to split a newborn night. There are three workable models, and the right one depends on who is actually available at night, not on what anyone thinks a fair split should look like on paper.

Template A is for a household where a partner is present and willing, and the mother is breastfeeding. The night is cut into named blocks. In the first block the partner covers the settling, the soothing and the nappy, and brings the baby to the mother only to feed, then returns the baby to the crib. In the second block the mother feeds, while the partner stays on call for resettles. In the third block the partner takes the baby entirely so the mother gets an uninterrupted stretch. The point is not that the mother stops feeding. The point is that the feeding is the only thing she owns for part of the night.

Template B is for formula or expressed feeding, where either adult can run a whole feed. You split the night into two long windows, and you alternate which adult owns which window on odd and even nights. The advantage is simplicity: one decision per night, already made. The cost is that each adult carries a full block alone - which is exactly why the next-night rotation matters.

Roster C is for the household where a partner works away, is often absent at night, or is simply not in the home. It does not pretend that a two-adult split is available. Instead it builds a support web: a relative, a friend, or paid help takes one slot, usually the early morning, so the mother gets one protected block through someone else. If there is genuinely nobody, the honest version protects the smallest possible block and drops everything that is not non-negotiable.

The routing question is one line: who is available at night? If a partner is present and willing, you are in A or B. If a partner is present but resists the whole idea, you start with A or B and one named shift only. If a partner works away or there is no partner in the home, you are in C. That is the whole decision tree - and it is written down so you do not have to rebuild it while tired.

Be honest about the cost of each. Template A asks the partner to stay awake and alert for a defined block - that is real work, and it is why the block is shorter than a full night. It asks the mother to hand the baby back rather than keep solving the night alone, which is often the harder habit to break. Template B asks each adult for a long solo stretch, so the rotation is not optional; skipping your night because you are tired is how the whole model collapses. Roster C asks you to accept help from outside the household, which is sometimes the hardest ask of all.

The most common mistake is choosing the fairest-looking model instead of the one that fits the household. A fifty-fifty split that requires a deep-sleeping parent to wake on a cry will fail within a week. A perfect-looking rotation breaks if one adult is travelling. Pick the model your real week can carry, not the model that reads best on paper - and if two models are close, pick the simpler one.

Whichever model you pick, it only exists once the clock times are written. Two times, one name per block, on paper. That is the difference between a template and a plan.
