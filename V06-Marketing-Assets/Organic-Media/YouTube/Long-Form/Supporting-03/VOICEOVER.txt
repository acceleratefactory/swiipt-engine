Deliver calmly, at a normal pace. No hype, no urgency, no advertisement tone.

Ask most couples why the night split never sticks and you get the same answer: he works tomorrow. It is a real constraint. It is not, however, a solution - and treating it as one is what kills the plan.

If the working parent always sleeps because they work tomorrow, then the other parent owns every night, permanently. That is not a compromise; it is a default that nobody wrote down. And it holds even though the parent at home is doing physically demanding, round-the-clock care with no protected sleep at all.

The shift is to plan the work night rather than exempt it. A working parent cannot usually own 1 AM to 5 AM on a work night. They can often own 9 PM to midnight, before their own sleep, or 5 AM to 7 AM, after it. A smaller, guaranteed block beats a large block that never actually happens.

So you name which block each adult realistically owns on a work night, and you write the clock times around the real shift. If the working parent leaves at seven, the late block belongs to them: they take the baby so the other parent gets a stretch before the hardest hours. If the working parent is home by six, the early block is theirs before bed. Either works. What does not work is an unspoken exemption.

The weekend is where the larger block goes. It is also where paybacks happen: the sleep banker exists precisely so that a night of extra load can be repaid within seven days - not remembered, not resented, repaid. A morning sleep-in on Saturday is a payback, not a favour.

There is one comparison worth making out loud, once, in daylight. The working parent's day has a start time and an end time. The at-home parent's day does not - it runs from the last feed of the night to the first feed of the next. That is not an argument for who suffers more; it is the reason a work schedule cannot be the only schedule in the house. Both timelines need to appear on the same page.

There will be weeks when work is unusually hard - a deadline, a trip, a difficult project. In those weeks you do not abandon the system; you shrink it. Drop to one guaranteed block: the smallest stretch the working parent can genuinely own. A three-hour block that actually happens protects more than a seven-hour block that exists only on paper. Then the following week, restore the fuller split.

Write the work-night blocks and the weekend block in the same place, with names and times. Then the question 'whose turn' has an answer before the week starts, and nobody has to argue for their own rest at midnight.
