## Life Area Market-Breakdown Method

Break down the Life Area using **exactly the same discipline and depth used for the Postpartum & New-Parent Life research. Do not stop at broad topics, conditions, demographics, or catchy product names.**

The required hierarchy is:M04 — Chronic-Condition Lifestyle Management 

Submarkets:

\

GERD IBS/low-FODMAP diabetic family cooking ADHD-related eating condition-specific organization meal planning symptom tracking

**Broad Life Area → Submarket → Specific Life Situation → Specific Person → Trigger → Failed Attempt / Constraint → Emotional Stake → Desired Transformation**

### 1. Start with the Broad Life Area

Identify the major domains within the Life Area.

Do not immediately generate products.

### 2. Break every domain into Submarkets

Go substantially deeper than the obvious categories.

Look for:

* overlooked submarkets
* underserved groups
* life-stage differences
* transition periods
* recurring problems
* culturally specific situations
* situational problems hidden inside broad categories
* problems created by changes in work, family, relationships, money, identity, environment, or lifestyle

Keep breaking down until the submarket reveals a **real-world situation**, not merely another topic.

### 3. Find the Specific Life Situation

This is the most important step.

Do NOT accept:

* "weight management"
* "stress"
* "confidence"
* "fitness"
* "financial planning"
* "relationship problems"
* "career development"

These are topics, not sufficiently scoped markets.

Instead identify the actual moment or recurring situation the person is experiencing.

Ask:

> **What is happening in this person's life that makes this problem urgent, concrete, and personally meaningful right now?**

### 4. Define the Specific Person

Do not settle for:

* men
* women
* parents
* professionals
* people over 40

Specify the person within the situation.

The person should be identifiable by their **life context**, not merely demographics.

### 5. Identify the Trigger

What caused the situation to become important now?

Examples:

* a life event
* deadline
* diagnosis
* transition
* failure
* embarrassment
* relationship change
* financial change
* career change
* family change
* milestone
* sudden realization
* recurring event

### 6. Identify the Failed Attempt / Constraint

What has this person already tried, expected to work, or been unable to do?

Look for:

* failed solutions
* conflicting advice
* previous attempts
* lack of time
* lack of money
* lack of support
* cultural constraints
* partner/family constraints
* physical limitations
* knowledge gaps
* emotional resistance
* environmental constraints

This is critical. Do not invent a failed attempt merely to complete the framework. If evidence is unavailable, mark it as unknown.

### 7. Identify the Emotional Stake

What does this situation actually mean to the person?

Go beyond:

* "they are frustrated"
* "they want to improve"

Find the deeper consequence:

* embarrassment
* fear
* loss of identity
* loss of confidence
* relationship strain
* shame
* feeling left behind
* fear of losing control
* fear of disappointing someone
* grief over a former self
* desire to regain independence
* desire to be respected
* desire to feel capable again

### 8. Define the Desired Transformation

State the transformation as:

**Before → After**

It must describe a meaningful change in the person's actual situation.

Do not define success as:

* consuming information
* finishing a guide
* learning about a topic

Define what is different in the person's life after the transformation.

---

## The Core Test

Every proposed submarket must eventually answer:

> **Who exactly is experiencing what specific situation, why is it happening now, what have they tried or what prevents them from solving it, what is at stake emotionally, and what meaningful change do they want?**

If you cannot answer those questions, **the opportunity is not sufficiently narrowed yet. Keep drilling down.**

### Important

**Do not use a catchy name to make a broad topic appear specific.**

A product title is NOT evidence of a niche.

For example:

❌ "Men's Fitness Reset"
❌ "Hormonal Health Guide"
❌ "Confidence After 40"

These remain broad even if the names sound compelling.

The underlying situation must be specific **before** naming the opportunity.

### Final Output

For every major submarket, continue drilling down and produce:

**Broad Topic**
→ **Submarket**
→ **Specific Life Situation**
→ **Specific Person**
→ **Trigger**
→ **Failed Attempt / Constraint**
→ **Emotional Stake**
→ **Desired Transformation**

Then continue searching for additional branches until you have exhausted the meaningful hidden submarkets.

**Do not prematurely turn the results into products. The objective at this stage is to map the opportunity landscape and discover the deepest, most specific situations.**

## ADDENDUM D — Strict Discipline Rules (Postpartum-grade)

The breakdown must pass the Postpartum standard, not merely contain the right field names. Apply to EVERY micro-situation.

### D1. Specific Person carries life context, not demographics
"Busy parent" / "working mother of two" is not specific enough. Specify the life context that makes the problem different: household role, children's ages/stage, arrival time, the window before the next obligation, who else is home.
- Weak: "A working mother of two."
- Strong: "Default household food-manager mother of two primary-school children, full-time job, home ~5:30pm, 30–45 min before homework/bath/bedtime."
The person layer must explain WHY this person's problem differs from another parent's — because the product opportunity may differ completely.

### D2. Failed Attempt / Constraint is MANDATORY in every micro-situation
No problem → emotion → outcome shortcut. Name the actual thing they tried that didn't survive real life, or the specific constraint blocking the obvious fix.
- Weak: "No ready snack." (a condition, not a failed attempt)
- Strong: "Bought snacks in bulk; kids finished them immediately or refused the healthier options."
For hidden markets especially, ask: what did they actually try? (planned all weekend meals? batch cooked? kept the weekday system? bought convenience food? enforced normal times?) The distinction changes the opportunity.

### D3. Emotional Stake reveals MEANING, not just feeling
Go past "guilt / stress / money guilt." State why it matters so much to THIS person.
- Weak: "Money guilt."
- Strong: "Every spoiled grocery makes her feel she failed at managing the family's money, because the household is already on a tighter budget."
The stake should expose the meaning of the problem, not only the emotion it produces.

### D4. Hierarchy integrity — classify every discovery at its correct level
Do not auto-promote a "hidden market" to a submarket. Each discovered item is one of: submarket / situation / life-stage or event situation / external constraint or trigger / coping behavior or consequence / cross-link / product opportunity. (M05 examples: Takeout creep = coping behavior; "What's for dinner" group-chat = situation inside Dinner Decision Fatigue; Grocery inflation = external constraint/trigger; Mental load / default-parent labor = genuine hidden submarket.) Put every discovery at the correct level or the tree becomes messy.

### D5. Do not define the problem by the solution
Seed labels like "Freezer Systems," "Food Chaining," "Grocery Organization" are solution-shaped. Ask: what is the person's actual life problem that happens to be addressable through X? One category contains multiple distinct buyers:
- Freezer Systems → (a) batch-cooks but can't find food Tue; (b) wants emergency meals for unpredictable workdays; (c) cooks for a dietary-restricted family member, needs separate inventory; (d) full freezer, no confidence what's usable. Same label, different buyers/triggers/stakes/products.
Never let the seed label become the problem definition.

### D6. Drill past the first micro-situation
For the strongest opportunities, keep going one level deeper. Example — Dinner Decision Fatigue: Sunday plan → Wed schedule change → forgot to thaw chicken → kids reject substitute → partner home at 8 → delivery ordered. The real opportunity may be a dinner decision engine that dynamically replans the remaining week when reality breaks the plan — invisible if you stop at "busy mom needs meal planning."

### D7. The 8-question test (impose on every candidate)
1. Broad topic? 2. Submarket? 3. Specific life situation (exact moment)? 4. Specific person (life context)? 5. Trigger (why now)? 6. Failed attempt/constraint (what tried/fails)? 7. Emotional stake (why it matters)? 8. Desired transformation (before→after)? If any answer is generic, keep drilling.

Keep `[MODEL INFERENCE]`/`[HYPOTHESIS]` labeling (Addendum C). Keep "decision removal, not recipes" as a hypothesis — do not conclude it prematurely; the next stage must test whether the transformation is one people will pay for.

## ADDENDUM E — Final Permanent Rules (from the M05 9/10 review)

Carry this exact standard into ALL remaining Life Areas (M06–M24). Do not regress to the 7.5/10 version.

### E1. Archetypal details must not masquerade as evidence
If a person detail is used to construct the archetype rather than derived from research, mark it `[HYPOTHESIS]`. E.g. "both anxious" is psychological characterization not established — tag it. Don't let specificity imply evidence.

### E2. Person must be specific enough to visualize a real day
No "busy parent" / "working parent" / "household with mixed needs" / "family with busy weekends" as the final person layer. Specific enough that we can imagine one real person's Tuesday.

### E3. The Tuesday Test
Could I describe this person's specific Tuesday without the words "busy," "stressful," "overwhelmed," "struggles"? If yes → concrete. If no → drill deeper. (Strong example: Wed planned chicken → forgot thaw → kids reject → partner late → delivery. Weak: "busy parent struggles with grocery organization.")

### E4. Transformation describes the changed life-state BEFORE the mechanism/product
Not "a freezer map + rotation." Use: From [stored but unusable] → To [reliably knowing what's available, where, what to use next]. The mechanism (map, list, OS) is a potential means, named after.

### E5. Tier rankings describe human opportunities, not prematurely chosen products
Tier labels name the human situation (e.g. "Dinner Decision Fatigue — 5:15pm plan failure"), keeping any proposed mechanism (e.g. dynamic replanning) inside the opportunity interpretation — so we don't get attached to the first product idea.

### E6. Separate markets from situations, behaviors, constraints, interventions (D4 is permanent)
A research agent may call "takeout creep" a hidden market — correct it to behavioral consequence under Dinner Decision Fatigue. "Weekend meal collapse" → recurring failure state within Meal Rotation. Keep the hierarchy clean across all Life Areas.

### E7. High-scrutiny separation for clinical-adjacent markets
Severe/selective eating (feeding-therapy level) is NOT a severity extension of toddler picky eating — treat as a separate high-scrutiny market (different buyers, urgency, safety, WTP, professional involvement, acceptable interventions). Keep safety language.

### E8. No marketing prose; observable → mechanism → meaning → transformation
We are building a Transformation Library discovery database, not copy. Bias toward observable situation → causal mechanism → emotional meaning → transformation. Remove beautiful phrasing that isn't load-bearing.

### E9. Don't over-drill
Drill until the 8 questions have defensible answers; stop. Deep-drill ONLY when the failure chain reveals a materially different opportunity (§1-A yes; §1-B no). Purpose is discrimination, not verbosity.
