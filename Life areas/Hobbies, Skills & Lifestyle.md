BUT THERE IS A MUCH DEEPER WAY TO ORGANIZE THIS DOMAIN

I would add a second axis to the research.

Because two people can want the exact same hobby for completely different reasons.

PERSON A

Learns photography because they love photography.

PERSON B

Learns photography because their children are growing up and they want to preserve memories.

PERSON C

Learns photography because they want a side income.

PERSON D

Learns photography because they moved somewhere beautiful and need a new social activity.

PERSON E

Learns photography because retirement left them without purpose.

Same hobby.

Five completely different markets.

That's why the life situation and trigger are more important than the activity itself.

THE 8 BIG “WHY” MARKETS

I would classify every hobby/skill opportunity against these:

1. I WANT TO BECOME SOMEONE

Identity.

“I want to be a photographer.”

2. I NEED TO BE ABLE TO DO SOMETHING

Competence.

“I need to cook.”

3. I NEED SOMETHING TO FILL MY LIFE

Purpose.

“I retired and need something meaningful.”

4. I NEED TO CONNECT WITH PEOPLE

Belonging.

“I moved here and don't know anyone.”

5. I NEED TO ESCAPE MY ROUTINE

Restoration.

“Work is consuming me.”

6. I WANT TO PRESERVE SOMETHING

Memory / culture.

“I want my children to know these traditions.”

7. I WANT TO MAKE MONEY

Economic transformation.

“People want to buy what I make.”

8. I WANT TO FINALLY DO THE THING I'VE ALWAYS WANTED

Deferred aspiration.

“I've wanted to learn piano for 20 years.”

These why-markets are potentially more valuable than the hobby categories themselves.

AND THE MOST IMPORTANT INSIGHT

I would not define this domain as:

“people looking for hobbies.”

That is too shallow.

The deeper market is:

People whose relationship with their time, identity, competence, creativity, community, or lifestyle has changed — and they are looking for an activity or skill to help rebuild the life they want.

That gives you a much larger transformation library.

For example:

Retirement → identity disruption → hobby discovery → new identity

Empty nest → empty time → creative restart → meaningful personal life

Relocation → social isolation → hobby/community → belonging

Divorce → loss of shared routines → independent hobbies → new lifestyle

Young adult → leaving home → missing life skills → practical learning → independence

Diaspora parent → cultural loss concern → heritage skills → family transmission

Busy parent → no personal time → micro-hobby → personal identity preservation

Creative hobbyist → friends want to buy → hobby-to-income → controlled side income

Long-term professional → burnout → restorative hobby → life outside work

That is the Hobbies & Skills equivalent of the Postpartum-grade breakdown.

And I would preserve one particularly important principle for this domain:

The hobby itself is often NOT the market. The life problem that makes the hobby suddenly important is the market.
